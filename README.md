# Anti-AI Slop

A standing instruction file for AI writing tools. It names the patterns that make output read as machine-generated, and tells the model what to do instead of each one.

Most anti-AI-writing advice stops at vocabulary. Ban "delve," ban "tapestry," and the output still reads synthetic, because the vocabulary was never the problem. The tells that survive a normal edit are structural: the shape of the argument, the rhythm of the sentences, the fake symmetry of a three-part list. This file catches both layers, and the structural section is the one that does the work.

## The three patterns

Every version of AI slop is one of these, and they get more expensive in order.

- **Performative.** Writing built to look smart instead of clear. Costs you the reader
- **Reductive.** The specific flattened into the category average. Costs you the idea
- **Flattery.** Your own position inferred and handed back with better grammar. Costs you your judgment

The third one is the dangerous one. The first two look like bad writing. This one looks like agreement.

One signal covers all three, and it shows up in you rather than on the page. Relief. If a draft comes back and your shoulders drop, slow down. That feeling usually means the draft told you what you already thought.

## Start here

1. Open [`anti-ai-slop.md`](anti-ai-slop.md) and read the Structural Tells section. Ten minutes, and it's the part you can't unsee afterward
2. Paste the whole file into Claude Projects, a Custom GPT, or a system prompt, wherever your standards persist across a body of work
3. For a one-off where the writing matters, paste it at the top of the thread instead

Then keep writing and let it catch things. The standing instruction at the top of the file tells the model to report which patterns it had to fix, so you learn the tells rather than outsourcing them.

## The frequency rule

One instance of any pattern can land. Two in the same piece is a yellow flag. Three or more is AI exhaust.

This matters more than a zero-tolerance reading would. Several of these patterns are legitimate moves used once. What makes writing read as machine-generated is density, not presence.

## Why this exists

Storied builds narrative operating systems: the language a company runs on, treated as infrastructure rather than decoration. A standards file the model checks against is the smallest useful piece of that, and it's the piece most people can install this afternoon.

Published in full because a standard only compounds when the other people drafting alongside you are working from the same page, and the fastest way there is to hand someone the standard rather than the corrections.
